The book has no audience. That is the first thing to understand about it. It is not a journal, though it may contain a date. It is not a planner, though it may contain a list. It is not a blog, though it may one day become one. It is a commonplace book: a private collection of passages, observations, questions, and small pieces of the world that seemed worth keeping.

The practice is old enough to have a Latin name. In Renaissance Europe, students were taught to gather "commonplaces" — memorable arguments, quotations, and examples — into notebooks so they could be reused in speech and writing. Erasmus recommended the habit. John Locke published a treatise on indexing one's notes. The method changed, but the impulse did not: to make a place in the mind by making a place on the page.

A commonplace book is not a record of what you have read. It is a record of what has read you. The distinction matters. A bibliography tells you which books you finished. A commonplace book tells you which sentences stopped you, which ideas you could not put down, which questions you kept returning to when you were supposed to be doing something else.

## What goes in

Almost anything, as long as it is yours to keep. A line from a poem. A sentence from a novel. A fragment of conversation overheard on a train. A recipe with a note about who made it. A diagram of how two ideas might be connected. A word you had to look up. A question you cannot answer yet. A description of the light at a particular hour.

The test is not whether the entry is important. The test is whether it caught your attention. Commonplace books are not archives of the significant; they are archives of the noticed. Over time, the pattern of what you notice becomes a portrait of your mind.

This is why copying by hand matters. Typing is faster, and a digital commonplace can work, but the hand slows you down. Slowness is not a side effect; it is part of the method. When you copy a sentence, you feel its rhythm. You notice which words carry the weight. You have time to disagree with it, or to add a line of your own. The passage becomes less like a saved link and more like something you have handled.

## The rules I actually keep

I have tried many systems. The ones that survive are the ones that are easy to keep and hard to ruin. These are the rules I return to:

**One book at a time.** Not one book per subject, not one for reading and one for ideas. A single book becomes a record of a period of life. Its disorder is useful; it shows you what you were thinking about alongside what you thought you were thinking about.

**Date every entry.** Not because the date is important, but because it creates a spine. Years later, the dates let you see the shape of your attention. They also remind you that the book is a record of time, not a database.

**Copy what stops you.** Not what you think you should find important. Not the passage everyone quotes. The one that made you look up from the page. If nothing stops you, write down why you kept reading.

**Add one line of your own.** A question, a disagreement, a connection, a memory. The line does not have to be profound. It only has to be yours. Without it, the book becomes a scrapbook of other people's thoughts. With it, it becomes a conversation.

**Index at the back.** A simple list of names, ideas, and pages. Locke's method was more elaborate, but the principle is the same: a book you cannot find your way back into is a book you will stop using. The index can be messy. It can be rebuilt. It should not be a reason to delay starting.

**Do not organise too early.** The urge to impose categories is strong, especially for people who like systems. Resist it for a while. Let the entries accumulate. The categories that matter will announce themselves later, and they will be more interesting than the ones you would have chosen at the beginning.

**Revisit, do not just accumulate.** A commonplace book is not a competition. The point is not to fill pages; it is to return. Once a month, open the book at random. Read a few entries. Notice what still feels alive and what has gone quiet. Sometimes an old note becomes the seed of a new essay. Sometimes it becomes a reminder that you have changed.

**Let gaps happen.** A book with empty months is not a failed book. It is a record of a life that had other things to do. The gap is part of the record.

## The temptation to make it content

There is a particular danger for anyone who writes or publishes: the commonplace book can become a performance. You start choosing entries with an imagined reader in mind. You copy a sentence because it would make a good post. You arrange the pages so they photograph well. The book becomes another surface for the self, and the privacy that made it useful disappears.

The remedy is not secrecy for its own sake. It is to keep at least one place where you are not trying to be understood. A commonplace book is a good candidate. It can be ugly. It can be incomplete. It can contain the same idea three times, in three different moods, without resolution. That is not a flaw. It is the point.

## A book that remembers

Marcus Aurelius' *Meditations* is often described as a private notebook, though it is more than a commonplace book. It was written by an emperor to himself, in fragments, across years. It repeats. It argues with itself. It was never intended for publication, and that is part of why it still feels alive. We are reading a mind in the act of trying to remember what it believes.

A commonplace book does something similar on a smaller scale. It remembers what you noticed before you knew why it mattered. It holds the raw material of a thought until you are ready to think it. It is a form of hospitality toward your future self: a place set at the table, in case you come back hungry.

You do not need a beautiful notebook. You do not need a system. You need a pen, a book, and the willingness to stop when something catches you. The first entry can be a single sentence. The second can be a question. The third can be the date, and nothing else.

Over time, the book will become a map of your attention. It will not tell you who you are. It will show you what you kept looking at. That is a more useful thing.
