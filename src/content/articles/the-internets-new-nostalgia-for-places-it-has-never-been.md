A stone village at dawn. A narrow street in Lisbon, washing strung between windows. A wooden cabin in a Norwegian valley, its roof heavy with snow. A Japanese train platform in the rain, empty except for one person with an umbrella. These are not places most of us have been. They are places we have seen, often many times, in images that circulate through the feed like a form of weather.

The comments underneath are always some version of the same sentence: *I want to live here.* Sometimes the commenter has visited. Often they have not. The longing is real, even when the place is not. It is a strange kind of homesickness for a home you have never had.

The writer John Koenig coined the word *anemoia* for nostalgia for a time you have never known. Something like anemoia is now attached to places. Call it elsewhere-nostalgia: a yearning for a landscape, a town, a pace of life that exists for us primarily as an image. It is not the same as travel desire, which is at least oriented toward going. It is more like a mood in search of a location.

## The world became a backdrop

There is a practical reason this feeling has become so common. The internet has made more of the world visible than ever before. We can see the interior of a house in Kyoto, the light on a beach in Patagonia, the morning routine of a stranger in a small town in the Dolomites. The images are often beautiful, and they are often made to be beautiful. They are composed, filtered, cropped, and timed for the hour when the light is kindest.

What they leave out is the life that makes a place a place: the commute, the leaking roof, the neighbour's dog, the week of grey weather, the complicated history, the people who were there before the camera arrived. The image is not a lie, exactly. It is a selection. But when thousands of selections point in the same direction, they create a picture of a world that does not exist anywhere in particular.

We then compare our actual surroundings to that composite. Our street is not a cobbled lane in Portugal. Our morning is not a slow breakfast on a terrace. Our life is full of errands and noise and unfinished things. The comparison is unfair, but it is almost automatic. The feed has given us a standard of place that no place can meet.

## The tourist and the screen

This has consequences beyond private melancholy. When a place becomes an image, visiting it can become an attempt to re-create the image. The itinerary is built around the viewpoints that already exist online. The camera comes out at the exact spot where the photograph was taken. The experience is judged by how closely it matches the version we have already seen.

The result is a strange loop. The image draws us to the place; the place is then rearranged, sometimes literally, to accommodate the image. Local economies adapt to the visitors who come for the picture. The picture becomes more real than the life it was meant to depict. Meanwhile, the people who live there are asked to perform the version of themselves that the visitors have come to see.

This is not a new story. Tourism has always shaped the places it touches. But the speed and scale are different now. A single video can send a hundred thousand people to a small beach or a quiet street. The place may be physically unchanged, but its social reality has been rewritten overnight.

## Longing is not the enemy

It would be easy to conclude that elsewhere-nostalgia is simply false consciousness, a distraction from the real work of loving where we are. That conclusion is too quick. Longing is one of the ways we become aware of what we lack. It can be a form of attention, a signal that something in our life is out of balance: too much speed, too little beauty, too few places to walk, too little contact with weather and trees.

The problem is not that we long for elsewhere. The problem is that we let the image answer the longing. The image says: *this is what a good life looks like.* It does not say: *what would a good life look like here, with these people, in this weather, with the resources you actually have?* That is a harder question, and it cannot be answered by a screenshot.

There is also a difference between nostalgia and memory. Memory is particular and imperfect; it belongs to a person and a place. Nostalgia is often general and idealised; it belongs to an image. The village we miss may never have existed. The slower life we imagine may be a projection of our own exhaustion. That does not make the exhaustion unreal. It makes the image a poor doctor.

## The discipline of the local

If there is an antidote, it is not to travel more or to travel less. It is to practise a different kind of attention to place, wherever you are. Learn the names of the trees on your street. Take the same walk at different times of day. Notice which shops change and which endure. Talk to the person who runs the corner shop. Sit somewhere without taking a photograph. Let a place become familiar enough to be boring, and then keep looking.

This is not an argument against curiosity about elsewhere. The world is vast, and there is real pleasure in encountering other ways of living. But the encounter is richer when it is not organised around a picture. The best travel does not confirm the image; it complicates it. It gives you the smell of the place, the sound of its ordinary mornings, the sense that it is a place where people live rather than a set that has been arranged for your arrival.

The same is true of home. The place you are in is not a failed version of somewhere else. It is a particular place with a particular light, a particular history, and a particular set of possibilities. It may not photograph well. It may not trend. But it is yours to notice.

The feed will keep offering us elsewhere. It will make it beautiful, and it will make it easy to want. The harder, slower work is to ask what the wanting is for — and then to look up from the screen and see what is actually in front of us.
