The first web page was handmade. It had to be. In 1991, Tim Berners-Lee put together a short document at CERN explaining what the World Wide Web was and how to use it. It was plain text and links: a title, a few paragraphs, a list of details. There was no template, no design system, no analytics. There was a person, a file, and a server.

That page is still online, in a recreation hosted by CERN. It is almost shockingly simple. And yet it contains the essential grammar of the handmade web: a document made by someone, for someone else, with links as an invitation to go further.

The web grew quickly, and much of what grew was commercial. But alongside the shops and the portals, there was another tradition: people making pages about their lives, their interests, their cats, their bands, their families. They were not publishers in the industrial sense. They were inhabitants.

## A place, not a platform

The first personal websites were often hosted on services that feel almost mythological now: GeoCities, Angelfire, Tripod. GeoCities launched in 1994 and let people build a "homestead" in a virtual neighbourhood. You chose a theme, wrote some HTML, uploaded a few GIFs, and suddenly you had an address on the web.

The results were messy, personal, and gloriously varied. There were fan pages, poetry archives, recipe collections, conspiracy theories, family photo albums, and sites devoted to obscure hobbies. There were guestbooks, webrings, hit counters, and animated "under construction" signs. The aesthetic was not refined. It was alive.

The webring is worth remembering. A group of sites on a related theme would link to one another in a loop; a small navigation bar at the bottom of each page let you go to the next or previous site. It was a form of curation without an algorithm. A person had decided that these sites belonged together, and you could follow the thread until you reached the end.

Blogs extended the tradition. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, tools like Blogger made it easier to publish regularly, and a culture of personal writing grew around links, comments, and blogrolls. RSS let readers subscribe to the sites they loved without giving their attention to a platform. The blogroll was a public list of neighbours. It was a small, human map of the web.

## What platformisation took

Then the platforms arrived. MySpace, Facebook, Twitter: profiles instead of pages, feeds instead of links, a universal interface instead of a thousand strange ones. The trade was clear and, for many people, worth it. You did not need to know HTML. You did not need to find an audience. You did not need to maintain a server. You could just sign up and be part of the crowd.

But something was lost in the trade. A profile is not a place. It is a shape the platform provides. You can change the colours, sometimes, and the header image, but the structure belongs to someone else. The feed decides what your friends see. The algorithm decides what counts as a good post. The terms can change, and your corner of the web changes with them.

The handmade web did not disappear. It retreated. It persisted in personal blogs, in small forums, in the corners of the internet that never became fashionable. In 2013, a project called Neocities launched as a deliberate successor to GeoCities, offering free static hosting and a community of personal sites. Static site generators made it possible to build a fast, hand-crafted site without a database. The "digital garden" idea revived an older metaphor: a site as a place that grows over time, tended by its owner.

## What makes a site handmade

The handmade web is not defined by its tools. You can hand-code a site in HTML, or use a static site generator, or write your own small CMS. You can use a template, as long as you make it yours. The question is not whether you typed every character. The question is whether the site belongs to a person.

A handmade site has a point of view. It has a beginning and an end. It has links that were chosen rather than generated. It may be ugly. It may be out of date. It may have a page that has not been touched since 2018, and that is part of its charm. It is not trying to maximise engagement, because it is not a business. It is trying to be a place.

This is not a purity test. The web is better because it is easy to publish, and many people who would never have learned HTML have found audiences through platforms. The handmade web should not become another way to feel superior. It is simply a reminder that the web can be a place you make, not only a place you visit.

## The economics of a small corner

There is a practical side to this. A personal website is cheap. A domain name costs a little each year; static hosting can be free or nearly so. The expensive part is time: the time to learn a tool, to write something, to keep it alive. That time is not equally available to everyone. A handmade web that ignores this becomes a hobby for the already comfortable.

But the barrier is lower than it looks. You do not need a grand project. You need one page. A title, a paragraph about what you are interested in, a few links to things you love. You can add another page later. You can change your mind. The site can be a notebook rather than a monument.

The most important link on a handmade site may be the one that goes somewhere else. The web was built on the idea that documents could point to other documents. A page that only talks about itself is a dead end. A page that sends you outward is part of a web.

## A small act of ownership

The history of the handmade web is not a story of decline and revival. It is a story of a practice that keeps returning because it meets a need the platforms cannot meet: the need to have a place of one's own. A place where the layout is yours, the words are yours, and the links are a gift rather than a growth strategy.

If you have been thinking about making a site, this is the nudge. Start with one page. Write one true sentence. Link to three things you actually like. Do not wait for the design to be perfect. The web is not a finished product. It is a place that gets made, a little at a time, by people who decide to make it.

The first page was handmade. The next one can be too.
