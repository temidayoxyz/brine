There used to be an edge to the internet. You would reach it at the bottom of a page, or at the end of a list of links, or after the last entry in someone's blogroll. The edge was not a failure. It was a shape. It told you that you had been somewhere, and that the somewhere had limits.

The feed removed the edge. It did this so smoothly that we barely noticed. One day we were clicking through pages that someone had made; the next we were scrolling through a surface that had been assembled for us, and would assemble itself again as soon as we reached the bottom. There is no bottom. There is only more.

This was sold as abundance, and in many ways it was. The feed is very good at giving us things: more music, more writing, more recipes, more faces. But it is also very good at giving us the same thing in slightly different forms. The algorithm has learned to guess, and what it guesses is usually a narrower version of what we already are.

## The end of the page

The first loss was structural. A page has a beginning, a middle, and an end. A feed has only a present tense. When you finish an article on a well-made website, you are returned to the world: the footer, the credits, the links to other places. When you finish a video in a feed, the next one has already begun. The absence of an ending is not an accident of design. It is the design.

We have written before about [the architecture of attention](/journal/the-quiet-architecture-of-attention/), and the feed is one of its most effective rooms. It removes the small decisions that might otherwise interrupt us: whether to continue, where to go next, whether to stop. Those decisions are tiring, which is why we hand them over. But they are also how we know what we want.

## Serendipity is not the same as surprise

The word serendipity gets used loosely online. A recommendation that feels unexpected is not necessarily serendipitous. Serendipity requires an outside: a person, a place, a tradition, a piece of luck that does not originate in your own history. It is the pleasure of being changed by something you did not choose.

A recommendation engine can produce surprise, but it cannot produce an outside. It works from the traces you have already left. It can interpolate between your past selves; it cannot introduce you to a stranger. The result can be delightful, and often is. But it is a mirror with better lighting, not a window.

The old web had more windows. A webring took you to a site that a stranger had made and another stranger had approved. A blogroll was a public statement of taste, updated by hand, full of dead links and accidental discoveries. A forum had regulars whose obsessions you learned to recognize. None of this was efficient. That was the point. The friction was where the outside got in.

## The feed knows what you like before you do

There is a flattering story about personalization: it understands you. It saves you time. It finds the things you would have wanted if only you had known how to look. This story is not entirely false. I have found music, essays, and small corners of the internet through recommendations that I would never have reached on my own.

But the flattery has a cost. When a system anticipates your preferences, you stop exercising them. Desire is not a fixed list of things you like; it is a muscle that develops through contact with the world. You discover what you want by wanting the wrong thing, by being bored, by following a link that turns out to be a dead end. A feed that pre-empts this process can leave you with a strange, passive feeling: well supplied, and somehow uninterested.

Something similar happens with taste. The feed offers the feeling of having found something, without the risk of having gone looking. It is possible to spend an hour being interested and end it with no idea what you actually care about.

## The public square became a series of rooms

There is another loss, harder to name. When everyone's feed is different, everyone's internet is different. We no longer share a front page, a newsstand, a set of links that a whole culture has seen. We share a set of formats and phrases, but not a common path through them.

This has obvious benefits. People who were never represented by the old gatekeepers can find each other. Niche interests can thrive. The monoculture was never neutral. But the old public web, for all its exclusions, had a quality of shared terrain. You could argue about the same thing because you had both walked past it. Now the argument arrives already shaped by the room you are standing in.

The feed is not a public square. It is a series of rooms, each one arranged around a version of you. The rooms are comfortable. They are also difficult to leave, because leaving means becoming someone the room has not met.

## The web was never a golden age

It would be easy to turn this into nostalgia, and nostalgia would be a mistake. The early web was not a paradise. It was slow, expensive, often ugly, and far from universal. It had its own gatekeepers, its own cruelties, its own spam. Many people were not welcome there, and many still are not welcome here.

The point is not that the old web was better. The point is that it had a different set of defaults. It assumed that you would make choices, that you would follow links, that you would leave. Those defaults shaped the kind of attention the web asked of us. The feed has different defaults: stay, scroll, let us decide.

Defaults are not destiny. They can be changed, in small ways and large. The question is whether we are willing to notice what we have traded for convenience.

## Rebuilding an edge

The antidote is not to delete everything and move to a cabin. It is to reintroduce a few edges. To read something that was chosen by a person rather than a model. To subscribe to a newsletter and open it when it arrives. To keep a list of sites you actually like and visit them on purpose. To follow a link out of the feed and not come back for an hour.

An RSS reader is a small, unglamorous act of this kind. You choose the sources; the sources do not choose you. The feed is finite because you made it finite. You can reach the end of it, and when you do, you can close the tab and go outside. That sounds like a small thing. It is not.

There is a deeper practice, too: making a place on the web rather than only consuming one. A page with your own links, your own words, your own small corner. The [handmade web](/journal/a-brief-history-of-the-handmade-web/) is not a solution to the platform economy, but it is a reminder that the web can be a place you inhabit rather than a current you are carried by.

The feed will keep guessing. It is very good at it, and it will get better. The question is whether we will keep choosing: what to read, who to listen to, where to stop. The edge is not a wall. It is the shape that makes a place a place.

At the bottom of this page there is a footer, and below that, nothing. You have reached the end. You can stay for a moment, or follow a link, or close the tab. The choice is yours. It is a small choice, but it is the kind the feed was designed to take away.
