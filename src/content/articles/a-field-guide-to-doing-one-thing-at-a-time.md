The desk in front of me has one object on it: a blue pen. The notebook is open, the coffee is going cold, and the phone is in another room. This is not a heroic scene. It is a small, slightly boring arrangement that I have come to trust more than any productivity system I have tried.

Doing one thing at a time sounds like the most obvious advice in the world. It is also one of the hardest practices to sustain. The difficulty is not intellectual. We know what to do. The difficulty is that the world is arranged to keep us from doing it, and our own minds have learned to treat interruption as a kind of company.

This is a field guide, not a doctrine. These are practices that help, most of the time, for some people. They are not a cure for a culture of distraction, and they are not a substitute for rest, care, or the structural conditions that make focus possible. But they are a place to begin.

## 1. Name the unit

"Work on the essay" is not a task. It is a category. Categories are easy to postpone because they have no edges. A unit has an edge: "Draft the opening paragraph." "Read the first ten pages." "Make the list of questions." "Edit the section about the library."

A unit should be small enough that you can see the end of it, and specific enough that you know when you have started. Write it down before you begin. The act of naming turns a vague intention into a place you can actually go.

## 2. Clear the runway

You do not need a perfectly clean desk. You need a surface that does not make decisions for you. Put away the things that belong to other tasks. Close the tabs that belong to other projects. If you cannot close them, put them in a separate window and move that window out of sight.

The point is not aesthetics. It is reducing the number of cues competing for the same moment. A stack of bills on the desk is not just paper; it is a small voice saying *you should be doing something else*. Give the voice somewhere else to live.

## 3. One screen, one task

If you work on a computer, the screen is the room. Make it a room with one door. Use full-screen mode. Hide the dock. Turn off notifications — not just the banners, but the badges, the sounds, the little red circles that turn a tool into a request.

This is harder than it sounds because many jobs require several windows at once. If that is your work, define the unit in terms of the windows: "Compare these two documents" is a task that legitimately needs two panes. The rule is not one window. The rule is that every window on the screen belongs to the thing you are doing.

## 4. Use a threshold

A threshold is a small ritual that marks the transition into the work. It can be almost anything: making tea, sharpening a pencil, opening a notebook, reading the last paragraph you wrote, putting on a particular playlist. The content does not matter. The repetition does.

The threshold is especially useful at the end, too. A closing ritual tells the mind that the work is over, even if it is unfinished. Close the document. Write one sentence about where you stopped. Turn the chair away from the desk. Without an ending, work leaks into the rest of the day and rest becomes a kind of low-grade guilt.

## 5. Let boredom be information

The urge to check something arrives long before you are actually stuck. It often arrives in the first few minutes, when the work is still difficult and the reward is still far away. The phone offers a quick exit. The exit is not the problem; the problem is that we take it before we have learned what the difficulty is.

When the urge comes, wait sixty seconds. Notice what it feels like in the body. Is it restlessness in the hands? A pressure behind the eyes? A sudden interest in the weather, the news, the inbox? Boredom is often a signal that you have reached the edge of your current understanding. That edge is where the useful work happens. Stay there a little longer.

## 6. Batch the shallow

Not everything deserves deep attention. Email, invoices, scheduling, and the hundred small administrative tasks of a life are real work, and they need time. The mistake is letting them colonise the whole day in small, continuous doses. A message answered at 9:14, another at 9:37, another at 10:02 — the day is gone, and nothing has been finished.

Set aside a window for the shallow work. Do it in a batch. Then close the door on it. The window can be short. What matters is that it has edges.

## 7. Stop at a clean edge

Ernest Hemingway is said to have stopped writing each day when he knew what would happen next. Whether or not the story is true, the practice is useful. Ending in the middle of a thought gives you a running start tomorrow. Ending at the end of a section gives you the harder task of beginning again.

This is not always possible. Some work ends when the shift ends, when the child wakes, when the body says enough. But when you do have a choice, leave a small thread hanging. The next session begins with a pull rather than a push.

## 8. Notice when multitasking is avoidance

Sometimes we do several things at once because we are bored. Sometimes we do it because we are afraid. The second kind is harder to see. We open a second tab while writing because we are not sure the sentence is good. We answer a message while reading because the reading asks us to change our mind. We keep the feed open because silence feels like being alone with something we do not want to face.

If you notice this, do not scold yourself. Ask a gentler question: *What is the feeling I am avoiding?* The answer may be boredom, anxiety, grief, or simply the discomfort of being a beginner. Naming it does not make it disappear, but it gives you a choice about what to do next.

## The permission to adapt

It is worth saying clearly: monotasking is easier for some people than others. Caregiving, chronic illness, neurodivergence, insecure work, shared offices, and a dozen other conditions shape what is possible. A parent working from home is not failing at focus when a child needs them. A person with ADHD is not lacking discipline when their attention moves. The practices above are tools, not tests of character.

The point is not to become a machine that produces one thing at a time. The point is to have moments when you are actually where you are. A single hour of undivided attention is not a productivity hack. It is a way of being present to your own life.

The blue pen is still on the desk. The coffee is cold now, but I drank it. The page is full. That is enough for today.
