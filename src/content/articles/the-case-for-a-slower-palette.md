There is a paint chip taped to the wall beside my desk. It has been there for eleven months. It is a soft, greyish green — the colour of the underside of a leaf, or the sea on a cloudy afternoon — and I still cannot decide whether it belongs in the room. In the morning it looks almost silver. At four o'clock, when the sun comes through the west window, it turns warm and slightly yellow. On rainy days it disappears into the wall entirely.

This is not indecision, exactly. It is a relationship. The chip and I are getting to know each other. I am learning how it behaves in different light, next to different objects, in different moods. A colour that looked perfect on a screen in March may be a stranger by November. The wall is teaching me something that the paint shop could not.

We have become very fast at choosing colours. A brand can be rebranded in a week. A room can be repainted over a weekend. A palette can be generated, tested, and discarded before lunch. The tools are extraordinary, and the abundance is real. But speed has a cost, and in colour the cost is usually depth.

## The trend cycle has a refresh rate

Colour has always moved in cycles. The pigments available to a Renaissance painter were not the pigments available to a Victorian wallpaper maker, and the colours of a decade are usually a reaction to the decade before. What is new is the speed. Trends used to be transmitted through materials, manufacturing, and magazines; now they are transmitted through images, instantly, to anyone with a screen.

The result is a palette culture that behaves like fashion. A shade is "in." It appears in a thousand feeds. It becomes a shorthand for a particular kind of life: the muted sage of the wellness interior, the acid green of the tech startup, the warm terracotta of the Mediterranean-inspired restaurant. Then it is "out," and the same shade looks tired, not because anything about the colour changed, but because the images around it did.

This is not a moral failure. Design has to respond to its moment; a palette that ignores the present can feel like a costume. But there is a difference between a palette that belongs to a moment and a palette that is merely about the moment. The first can age. The second is designed to be replaced.

## A slow palette is not a beige palette

The phrase "slow palette" may suggest restraint, neutrals, and a lot of oatmeal. That is one version, but it is not the only one. A slow palette can be saturated, eccentric, even loud. What makes it slow is not its temperature. It is the length of the relationship.

A slow palette is chosen for how it will look after the novelty has worn off. It is tested in the actual light of the actual room, next to the actual objects that will live there. It is allowed to be slightly wrong. It is built from things that carry memory: a inherited plate, a length of cloth, a stone from a beach, a colour you have loved since childhood and cannot entirely explain.

The artist and writer David Batchelor has spent much of his career arguing that colour is not a decorative add-on but a fundamental way we make sense of the world. He is wary of colour reduced to branding or a scheme. A palette, in that spirit, is closer to a vocabulary: something you use to say what you mean, not something you apply to make a surface look finished.

## The screen is a poor place to meet a colour

One reason palettes date so quickly is that we choose them on screens. A screen emits light; a wall reflects it. A screen has a white point that changes with the hour and the device; a room has a particular quality of daylight that no colour profile can capture. The same hex code can look entirely different on a phone, a laptop, and a printed page.

This is not an argument against digital colour. It is an argument for testing it in the world. Print a swatch. Paint a board. Live with it for a week. Look at it at the time of day when you will actually use the room. Notice what happens beside wood, linen, skin, and the other colours that are already there.

For digital products, the constraints are different but the principle is the same. A palette has to work in dark mode and light mode, at small sizes, for people with low vision, on cheap screens and expensive ones. Accessibility is not a limitation imposed on colour; it is part of what colour is for. A palette that only works in a mockup is not a palette. It is a picture of one.

## Choose fewer colours, and let them work

One of the most useful constraints is a small number. Not because minimalism is morally superior, but because a limited palette forces decisions. If everything can be any colour, nothing has a role. When you have three or four colours, you have to decide which one carries the structure, which one carries the accent, and which one is allowed to be quiet.

A useful exercise is to name the colours by what they do rather than what they are. Not "sage" and "terracotta," but "ground," "ink," "signal," and "rest." The names are not important; the roles are. A palette with roles can survive changes of taste. A palette without roles is just a collection.

This is how the visual language of Brine was built: a warm ivory ground, a deep green-black ink, a sea-glass accent, and a single darker band for moments that need weight. The palette is small on purpose. It gives the journal a recognisable atmosphere without asking every page to shout. It also makes the occasional strong colour — a photograph, a link, a rule — feel like an event.

## Colour as a relationship

The chip is still on the wall. I think it is going to stay. Not because it is the most fashionable green, or the most impressive one, but because it has become part of the room's weather. I know how it looks in the morning now. I know what it does to the light.

That is the case for a slower palette: not that it is more tasteful, but that it is more yours. It is a record of attention. It says that you were here long enough to notice how the colour changed.

The next time you choose a palette, whether for a room, a brand, or a page, try living with it a little longer than feels comfortable. Put away the trend report. Turn off the screen. Look at the colour in the place where it will actually live. You may discover that the right choice was not the one that looked best in the image. It was the one that looked most like home.
